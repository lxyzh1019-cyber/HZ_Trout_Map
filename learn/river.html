A river is easier to read than a lake: the current shows you where the food goes. Trout and whitefish want three things: food carried to them, a break from the push of the water, and cover from birds and anglers. Find water that gives all three, and you have found fish. The example here is the Red Deer River below Dickson Dam, west of Innisfail.

The regulations are the only legal source. River rules differ by stretch and by season: check the open season, bait rule and limits for the Red Deer River before you go. Bull trout are protected: release every one.

The example: Red Deer River below Dickson Dam

Below the dam, the water comes from deep in Gleniffer Reservoir. It stays cold and fairly clear all summer, so trout do well here when other prairie rivers get too warm. Brown trout and mountain whitefish are the main fish; there are some rainbow trout, and bull trout pass through.

The dam sets the flow. A release can raise the river fast and make a gravel bar an island. Watch the water level, and never cross to a bar you cannot wade back from.

How to read the current

  • Fast water has a broken, choppy top. It carries food but tires fish out.
  • Slow water is smooth and glassy. Fish rest there but less food comes by.
  • The best water is where the two meet. Look for the line where fast and slow water touch: bubbles, foam and drifting leaves gather on it.
  • Darker water is deeper. Polarized glasses let you see the colour change and the rocks under it.
  • Anything that breaks the current — a rock, a log, a bridge pier, a bend — makes a soft spot just behind or beside it.

Where the fish hold, by water type

  • A — Riffle: shallow, broken water over gravel. Insects live under these stones. Trout feed at the head and tail of riffles at dawn, at dusk and in a hatch. Whitefish like the deeper riffles.
  • B — Run, and its seam: a smooth, steady stretch 1–2 m deep below a riffle. The seam, the line between fast and slower water, is the best place to drift a nymph or a fly. Brown trout lie just on the slow side of it.
  • C — Eddy behind a rock: the water swirls back upstream behind a boulder. Food circles in it. Cast just above the rock and let the fly swing into the soft water.
  • D — Pool: the deep, slow water on the outside of a bend. Big trout rest here at midday and in cold weather. Fish it slow and deep, and fish the head where the current enters.
  • E — Undercut bank: on the outside of a bend the current cuts under the bank. It gives shade, cover and food falling from the grass. Big brown trout live here. Drift a streamer or a hopper tight along it.
  • F — Tailout: the pool gets shallow again before the next riffle. Fish move up into it to feed in the evening. Approach slowly: they see you easily in shallow, smooth water.
  • G — Inside bar: the gravel on the inside of the bend. The water along its edge is slow. Stand on the bar and fish across to the deeper water and the seam. Do not wade on it after a release.

By season and water

  • Spring (after ice-off to June): runoff from tributaries can colour the water. Fish the slower, clearer edges and the seams. Stoneflies and mayflies begin on warm afternoons.
  • Summer: fish early and late; at midday move to the deeper runs and the undercut banks. Caddis come at dusk. Grasshoppers along grassy banks in late summer.
  • Fall: brown trout get aggressive before they spawn: streamers along banks and in pools. Give spawning fish on gravel beds space, and do not walk on the redds.
  • Winter, where the season is open: the dam keeps some water open. Fish are slow and deep in the pools and slow runs. Whitefish feed on small nymphs. Ice shelves along the bank can break: stay off them.
  • High or dirty water: fish move to the edges, out of the push. Fish close to shore, in eddies and behind points, with bigger, darker flies or lures.
  • Low, clear water: fish are shy. Use a longer, lighter leader, small flies, and keep low and back from the edge.

Safety

  • The flow can rise fast after a release from the dam. Wade only where you can walk back, and watch the water against a rock.
  • Wear a life jacket and a wading belt. Cold water saps strength in minutes.
  • Never cross to a gravel bar you could not get back from if the river came up.
