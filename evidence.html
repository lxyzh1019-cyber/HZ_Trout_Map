What the conditions score is built on

And, just as much, what it is not built on.

Most fishing calendars hand you one number and ask you to trust it. The number usually blends sun, moon, and barometric pressure as though the evidence behind them were comparable. It is not. Two of those have a mechanism you can point at; the other two are weak, and one of them has been measured and found to be worth about five percent. This page says which is which, and the score on the map is weighted to match — not to match convention.

The short version. Light and water temperature carry the score. Pressure is included as a proxy for an approaching weather front, not because fish sense the barometer. The moon is a tiebreaker capped at ±5%, and it can never move or suppress the dawn and dusk peaks.

The weights, and why

FactorWeightEvidenceBasis
Light / sun0.40Strong Salmonids are demonstrably crepuscular. Light intensity directly governs prey detection and predation success in trout, and it is computable to the minute with no network at all.
Water temperature0.35Strong Fish are ectotherms, so metabolic rate drives digestion and therefore appetite. Species thermal optima are well published. The weak link is our estimate of the temperature, not the physiology — so the tier shown on the map drops when the number is modelled rather than measured.
Pressure trend0.15Moderate A front-arrival proxy. See below — the mechanism is the front, not the barometer.
Wind0.06Moderate Chop cuts surface visibility and pushes food onto the windward shore. Also a safety input.
Precipitation0.04Weak Mostly comfort and safety rather than fish behaviour.
Moon×0.95–1.05Weak Applied after everything else, and hard-capped. See below.

Any factor whose input is missing is dropped and the rest are renormalised. A missing input never becomes an invented default — offline, the score is honestly labelled as coming from sun and moon alone.

Pressure: the arithmetic that decides it

The usual story is that falling pressure expands a fish's swim bladder and makes it uncomfortable. That story does not survive the numbers.

One hectopascal of air pressure equals 1.02 cm of fresh water. A dramatic frontal swing of 30 hPa is therefore about 31 cm of depth-equivalent — less than a trout experiences drifting up off a shoal. A fish that rises one metre puts itself through three times the pressure change of a major storm, deliberately, without any apparent distress.

Controlled work agrees. A feeding trial on yellow perch found no significant effect of barometric pressure on how much the fish ate; the apparent trend in the data turned out to be the fish acclimating to the laboratory.

So why is pressure in the score at all? Because falling pressure is a reliable sign that a front is coming, and a front brings cloud, wind, a change in light and a change in temperature — all things that do affect feeding. Guide logbooks that report better fishing on a falling barometer are, on this reading, correct about the pattern and wrong about the cause. We score the front and label it as such. Nowhere in this app does a swim-bladder explanation appear.

The moon, and why a major can land at three in the afternoon

Solunar tables mark major periods (about two hours) centred on the moon's transit — when it is directly overhead — and its anti-transit, when it is underfoot on the far side of the Earth. Minor periods (about one hour) centre on moonrise and moonset.

These are pure geometry and have nothing to do with light. The lunar day is 24 h 50 m, so transit slides about fifty minutes later each calendar day and sweeps every hour of the clock over a month. The phase tells you where it sits:

A major in the middle of the afternoon simply means you are a few days off new or full moon. It is not a claim that the fish skipped dawn. Calendars that add the sun and moon into a single ranking blur exactly this, which is how an app ends up telling you to fish at two in the afternoon in July.

This one keeps them apart. Sun and moon are drawn as two separate tracks on the same timeline, and the moon enters the score only as a multiplier applied after the weighted sum. The light score is never handed a lunar quantity of any kind.

Why ±5% specifically

Because that is what the largest study of the question found. Vinson and Angradi (2014) analysed 341,959 angler-caught muskellunge and found a real, consistent 29-day lunar signal in the catch — with a maximum relative effect of about five percent. Fishing only on the peak lunar day yields roughly five percent more fish than fishing on random days. The effect was stronger for large fish, in midsummer, and at high latitudes, which is mildly encouraging for Alberta.

Five percent is real and worth showing. It is not worth letting it reorder your day. Since a dusk slot scores above 0.90 and a midday slot below 0.45, a ±5% rescale cannot swap them: the best window of the day is lunar-invariant by construction, and that is asserted in the self-check below rather than merely intended.

What this cannot know

  • The actual water temperature. Free weather services report air. Surface water is estimated from a smoothed week of air temperature, which is a published approach but carries roughly ±2 °C, and more on small shallow water or just after ice-off. Anything modelled is shown dimmed and marked as modelled. If you measure it, type it in and it wins.
  • What is happening below the surface. In midsummer a deep lake separates into layers and the fish sit below the warm top. Surface temperature then describes water the trout are deliberately avoiding.
  • Whether the fish are there at all. Stocking records say what was put in and when. They cannot say what survived the winter.
  • Anything about your particular lake on your particular day. The honest ceiling on all of this is modest. Environmental variables of this kind typically explain a minority of the variation in catch. Treat the score as a way to choose between Saturday and Sunday, not as a forecast of a fish.
